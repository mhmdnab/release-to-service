The system behind the signature

European continuing airworthiness rests on one Commission Regulation. Its annexes, the “Parts”, divide the work: who trains, who holds a licence, who may maintain, and who manages airworthiness. Every Member State’s authority applies the same text.

Three layers of rules

Hard law → soft law

Basic RegulationReg. (EU) 2018/1139

Adopted by the European Parliament and Council. Sets the essential requirements for aviation safety and gives EASA its mandate.

Implementing rulesReg. (EU) No 1321/2014

Adopted by the Commission and binding in every Member State. Its annexes are Part-M, Part-145, Part-66, Part-147 and the rest.

AMC & GMEASA ED Decisions

Acceptable Means of Compliance show one accepted way to meet a rule. Guidance Material explains what the rule means. An organisation may use an alternative means of compliance if its authority agrees.

How we got here

    The Joint Aviation Authorities publish JAR-145, JAR-66 and JAR-147, harmonised codes the EU later turns into law.

  2. 2003Reg. (EC) 2042/2003

    Part-M, Part-145, Part-66 and Part-147 become EU law. EASA begins operating.

  3. 2014Reg. (EU) 1321/2014

    A recast of the continuing airworthiness rules becomes today’s base text.

  4. 20182018/1139 · 2018/1142

    A new Basic Regulation is adopted, and the B2L and L licence categories join Part-66.

  5. 2020Part-CAMO · Part-CAO

  6. 2022Reg. (EU) 2021/1963

  7. 2024Reg. (EU) 2023/989

    A major update to Part-66 and Part-147 adds a revised module structure and distance learning.

  8. 2026Reg. (EU) 2023/203

    Part-IS brings information security management to Part-145 from 22 February 2026.

ANNEX IV · PART147

Training organisations

Part-147 approves the schools. A maintenance training organisation (MTO) can run approved basic training courses, aircraft type or task training, and the examinations that count towards a Part-66 licence.

What an approved MTO needs

Subpart B · organisational requirements
147.A.105

Accountable manager & compliance

An accountable manager with corporate authority to finance every training commitment, and nominated person(s) responsible for compliance with Part-147.

147.A.105

Instructors, examiners, assessors

Enough staff to deliver training, knowledge exams and practical assessments. One person may hold several roles. Instructors and examiners get updating training at least every 24 months.

147.A.100

Facilities

Enclosed classrooms separate from other facilities, workshops for practical training, no more than 15 students per instructor or assessor in practical work, a technical library and secure record storage.

147.A.115 / .120

Equipment & material

Instructional equipment, access to examples of the aircraft type for type courses, and training material that covers the Part-66 syllabus at the right knowledge level.

147.A.125 / .130

Records & quality

Training, exam and assessment records are kept for an unlimited period. Training procedures and a quality system monitor standards and compliance.

147.A.140

The exposition (MTOE)

This document describes the organisation, its courses, staff and procedures, and the authority audits against it. The approval certificate is EASA Form 11.

Basic knowledge exams are held by a Part-147 organisation or by the competent authority. For category L, another organisation agreed with the authority may also hold them (66.A.25(b)).

Type training & OJT

Subpart D · Part-66 Appendix III
Level1

Brief overview of the airframe, systems and powerplant, as in the systems description section of the maintenance manual.

Level2

Basic system overview: controls, indicators and principal components with their location and purpose, plus servicing and minor troubleshooting.

Level3

Detailed description, operation, component location, removal and installation, BITE and troubleshooting to maintenance-manual level.

Type courses (147.A.300) have theoretical and practical elements and end with a type evaluation (147.A.305). The Appendix III syllabus sets a training level for each ATA chapter and licence category. Task training for category A covers specific tasks instead of a whole type.

On-the-job training (OJT) is required for the first type rating in each licence category or subcategory (not for C):

  • Completed within the 3 years before applying for the rating
  • At least half of the tasks done after the theoretical type training
  • Ends with a final assessment lasting at least one working day
  • No fixed minimum duration: the task list sets the length

For Group 2 and 3 aircraft, a type rating can also be endorsed after a type evaluation plus demonstrated practical experience on the type (66.A.45(d)).

ANNEX III · PART66

Engineer licensing

      Aircraft groups & ratings

      66.A.5 groups · 66.A.45 endorsement

      Group 1

      Complex motor-powered aircraft, multi-engine helicopters, non-piston aeroplanes certified above FL290, fly-by-wire aircraft, and gas airships other than ELA2.

      B1 · B2 · C: individual type rating

      Group 2

      2a single turboprop aeroplanes, 2b single turbine-engine helicopters, 2c single piston-engine helicopters, when not in Group 1.

      B1 · B2 · C: type, manufacturer subgroup or full subgroup. B2L: manufacturer subgroup or full subgroup

      Group 3

      Piston-engine aeroplanes not in Group 1.

      B1 · B2 · C: type or full group. B2L: full group. B3: its own single rating

      Group 4

      Sailplanes, powered sailplanes, balloons and airships not in Group 1.

      B2 · B2L: full group. L: rating per subcategory

      For B1 and C, a manufacturer subgroup rating requires qualification on at least two types from one manufacturer, and a full subgroup rating on at least three types from different manufacturers (66.A.45(e)). Category A needs no aircraft rating on the licence.

      Licence vs authorisation

      Two documents, two issuers

      PART-66 · ISSUED BY THE AUTHORITYThe licence

      • EASA Form 26, held personally and recognised in all EASA states
      • Lists categories, subcategories, aircraft ratings and any limitations
      • Becomes invalid 5 years after issue or last change unless submitted to the authority for verification (66.A.40)
      • Using its privileges requires 6 months of relevant experience in the preceding 2 years, adequate competence and command of the maintenance data’s language (66.A.20(b))

      PART-145 · ISSUED BY THE ORGANISATIONThe certification authorisation

      • Written scope set by the organisation, within your licence and its approval (145.A.35)
      • Minimum age 21, with competence assessed, including human factors
      • 6 months of relevant experience in any 2 consecutive years
      • Recurrent training in every 2-year period
      • Valid only while you work for that organisation

      From student to signature

      The typical sequence

      1. Choose a category

        A, B1, B2, B2L, B3, C or L. Each has its own modules, experience and privileges.

      2. Pass the modules

        Take a Part-147 course with its exams, or self-study and sit the exams with the competent authority.

      3. Build experience

        Work 1 to 5 years on operating aircraft, depending on the category and your training route.

      4. Apply for the AML

        Send EASA Form 19 to the authority and receive the licence on EASA Form 26.

      5. Type training

        Complete theoretical and practical training on your first aircraft type, with a type evaluation.

      6. On-the-job training

        Do supervised tasks on the type. This is required for the first type rating in each category.

      7. Rating endorsed

        The authority adds the aircraft rating to your licence.

      8. Get authorised

        A Part-145 organisation assesses you and issues a certification authorisation.

      9. Sign the CRS

        Certify that the work was done correctly and the aircraft is ready for release to service.

      Line & base maintenance

      Every A rating is limited to one or both

      BEFORE FLIGHTLine maintenance

      Maintenance done before flight to make sure the aircraft is fit for the intended flight. It covers troubleshooting, defect rectification, component replacement (with external test equipment if needed), and scheduled checks with visual inspections that catch obvious problems without extensive in-depth inspection.

      Certified by B1, B2, B2L, B3 or L staff within their privileges, or by category A for endorsed tasks.

      IN THE HANGARBase maintenance

      Anything beyond line maintenance: heavy checks, detailed structural inspections, major modifications and repairs, usually with the aircraft out of service for days or weeks.

      For large aircraft, a category C engineer issues the release for the aircraft as a whole. B1 and B2 support staff first confirm that the tasks in their specialty were properly completed.

      People & resources

      145.A.25 – 145.A.45
      145.A.30(a)

      Accountable manager

      Has corporate authority to fund and carry out all maintenance to the required standard, and sets and promotes the safety policy.

      145.A.30(b)–(cb)

      Nominated persons

      Lead maintenance, compliance monitoring and safety management, with direct access to the accountable manager.

      145.A.30(d)

      Man-hour plan

      Ensures enough qualified staff to plan, perform, supervise, inspect and monitor the work, with a procedure to reassess work when staffing drops below plan.

      145.A.35

      Certifying & support staff

      Assessed, authorised in writing, at least 21 years old, with 6 months of relevant experience in any 2 consecutive years and recurrent training every 2 years.

      145.A.25 / .40

      Facilities & tools

      Hangars for base maintenance, protected workshops, and segregated storage for serviceable and unserviceable parts. Tools and equipment are calibrated to an officially recognised standard.

      145.A.42 / .45

      Components & data

      Parts are accepted only with the right release, such as EASA Form 1, and properly classified. Work follows current maintenance data: ADs, instructions for continued airworthiness and approved repair data.

      Signing off the work

      145.A.48 · 145.A.50 · 145.A.55 · 145.A.60

      A certificate of release to service is issued before flight when maintenance is complete. Authorised certifying staff sign it on behalf of the organisation after verifying that all ordered maintenance was properly done to the exposition’s procedures and the maintenance data, with no known non-compliance that endangers flight safety.

      A component maintained off the aircraft is released on EASA Form 1, the authorised release certificate. New defects or incomplete work orders go back to whoever manages the aircraft’s continuing airworthiness for agreement.

      After any critical maintenance task, the organisation must apply error-capturing methods such as an independent inspection (145.A.48).

      Management system

      145.A.200 · 145.A.200A · 145.A.202

      Since 2 December 2022 (Reg. (EU) 2021/1963), every Part-145 organisation must run a management system. It combines a safety policy and objectives, hazard identification and safety risk management, an internal safety reporting scheme that protects reporters, compliance monitoring, and safety training and communication.

      From 22 February 2026, Part-IS adds an information security management system (145.A.200A). It covers cyber and information risks that could affect aviation safety.

      The Maintenance Organisation Exposition (145.A.70) is the organisation’s rulebook. It includes the accountable manager’s statement and safety policy, nominated persons and the organisation chart, certifying staff, man-hour resources, facilities and scope of work, the procedure for notifying changes, and all maintenance and management system procedures. The authority audits against it.

      Oversight & findings

      Section B · 145.B.305 · 145.B.350
      Level 1 finding

      A significant non-compliance that lowers safety or seriously hazards flight safety. The authority acts immediately to limit, suspend or revoke the approval.

      Level 2 finding

      Any other non-compliance. The organisation gets a corrective action period of up to 3 months at first, which can be extended if a corrective action plan is agreed.

      Oversight cycle24 mo

      Standard audit planning cycle. It can be extended to 36 months if set conditions are met, or 48 months with continuous reporting to the authority.

      The dirty dozen

      Module 9 · human factors

      These are twelve common preconditions for maintenance error, first described by Gordon Dupont at Transport Canada. Each comes with a safety net.

      Lack of communicationGive clear, written shift handovers. Never assume.
      ComplacencyExpect to find a fault. Sign only for what you saw.
      Lack of knowledgeUse current data and ask when you’re unsure.
      DistractionMark where you stopped, then restart a few steps back.
      Lack of teamworkShare the plan. Everyone owns safety.
      FatigueKnow the signs and get an independent inspection.
      Lack of resourcesPlan parts and tools ahead. Don’t improvise.
      PressureSpeak up. The schedule never outranks safety.
      Lack of assertivenessRefuse to accept a standard you know is wrong.
      StressStep back, take a breath and think it through.
      Lack of awarenessAsk what else this job could affect.
      NormsFollow the procedure, not the shop-floor shortcut.

      Maintenance Organisation Exposition: the Part-145 organisation’s approved manual (145.A.70).
      MPD
      Maintenance Planning Document from the type certificate holder.
      MTO / MTOE
      Maintenance Training Organisation and its exposition under Part-147.
      MTOM
      Maximum Take-Off Mass, used to draw the lines between ratings and licences.
      NDT
      Non-destructive testing, e.g. eddy current, ultrasonic or X-ray inspection. Part-145 rating D1.
      OJT
      On-the-job training for the first type rating in a licence category or subcategory.
      Part-IS
      EU information security requirements, applicable to Part-145 organisations from 22 February 2026.
      SB
      Service Bulletin: a manufacturer’s recommended inspection or modification.
      SMS
      Safety Management System, part of the Part-145 management system since December 2022.
      Support staff
      B1/B2 staff in base maintenance who confirm tasks in their specialty before the category C release.
      TC / STC
      (Supplemental) Type Certificate: the design approval that maintenance data flows from.
      Type rating
      An aircraft rating on the licence that allows certification on a specific type.

      Frequently asked

      Plain answers
      Is my EASA licence valid in every European country?

      It is recognised in all 31 EASA member states: the 27 EU countries plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland. Your employer still issues its own certification authorisation, and you must be able to work in the language of the maintenance data. Since Brexit, the UK CAA issues its own UK Part-66 licences, and the two systems no longer recognise each other automatically.

      Do I have to attend a Part-147 school?

      No. You can study on your own and sit the module exams with the competent authority. An approved basic course shortens the experience you need, for example from 5 years to 2 for B1.1 or B2.

      Can a B2 engineer release an engine change?

      No. Mechanical work on the powerplant is B1 territory. A B2 covers avionic and electrical systems, plus electrical and avionics tasks within powerplant and mechanical systems that need only simple tests to prove serviceability.

      What is the difference between B1/B2 and C?

      B1 and B2 engineers certify line maintenance and act as support staff for their tasks in base maintenance. A category C engineer issues the release to service after base maintenance, for the aircraft in its entirety.

      My module exams are old. Do they still count?

      Each module must have been passed within 10 years before you apply for the licence or add a category. Older passes can still be credited by the authority through an examination credit report. Modules already used for a licence you hold don’t expire.

      Is EASA Part-147 the same as FAA Part 147?

      No, despite the shared number. FAA 14 CFR Part 147 certifies aviation maintenance technician schools for the US A&P certificate, and FAA Part 145 covers repair stations. The EU–US bilateral agreement lets each side accept the other’s repair stations under special conditions, but the licences are not interchangeable.

      How long does it take to certify on an airliner?

      With an approved Part-147 B1.1 or B2 course (at least 2 400 hours), the legal minimum is 2 years of practical experience. Without formal technical training, it is 5 years. After that you still need type training, OJT on your first type, and an authorisation from a Part-145 organisation before you sign your first CRS.
